# Extraordinary circumstances and claim handling

## The exemption

An airline does not owe compensation if the disruption was caused by extraordinary circumstances that
could not have been avoided even if all reasonable measures had been taken. The airline carries the burden
of proof. The exemption removes the right to compensation only; care and assistance obligations remain.

## Typically accepted

- Severe weather that makes the flight unsafe, such as storms, heavy snow or volcanic ash.
- Air traffic control restrictions or strikes by air traffic controllers.
- Airport closures and security risks.
- Hidden manufacturing defects reported by the aircraft maker, and bird strikes.

## Typically not accepted

- Ordinary technical faults found during routine maintenance or pre-flight checks.
- Strikes by the airline's own staff.
- Crew shortages and late arrival of the aircraft from an earlier flight, unless that earlier delay was
  itself caused by extraordinary circumstances.
- Overbooking.

## Making a claim

The claim goes to the operating carrier, which is the airline that ran the flight, not necessarily the one
that sold the ticket. Useful evidence is the booking confirmation, boarding pass, proof of the actual
arrival time, and any notice of cancellation. The time limit for claiming depends on national law and
ranges from two to six years, depending on the country where the claim is brought. If the airline refuses
or ignores the claim, the passenger can complain to the national enforcement body or go to court.
